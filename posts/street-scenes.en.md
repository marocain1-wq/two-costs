# Street Scenes

There's nothing that comes from nothing. And here I am. That's who I became — but I became it in Australia, and now I'm trying to be that person here, in the country I was born in. Someone who spent forty years learning to be completely self-sufficient runs into a particular kind of trouble in a place that runs on interference — and the trouble gets sharper when that place is also home. In Australia, if something goes wrong, there's no one to blame but me. That country gave me the room to build my life exactly the way I wanted. Here, interference is a daily fact, not an exception. Five trips in three years, and the paperwork still isn't finished. Morocco has taught me a kind of patience I never needed in Australia — where the only thing worth queuing for is the barista.

Essaouira itself has changed. It used to be a quiet fishing town, before the tourists found it. I was sitting in a café when it hit me: where is the haik? That long white wrap you see in every old postcard of this place — gone, apparently, the way of the fax machine. I hadn't seen one, not once, in any of my last visits. Housing and daily costs have climbed the way I've read they have in Barcelona, in Venice — the locals selling up and moving out to the countryside, leaving behind the ones running the businesses that serve who's left. Inside the old walls, at this time of year, there are more tourists than Moroccans. I still haven't found a way to just walk past a child selling tissues for the family income and not carry it with me for the rest of the day.

And there's a discrepancy I can't stop noticing — between what people say in public and what I watch happen in private, in my own family, among people I've known my whole life. I'm not making a judgment about anyone. I'm just Moroccan, living inside it, and I don't have a clean answer for the gap.

Money has changed things since I left. Getting through a day here without some current of uncertainty running underneath it — I'm still not used to that. Withdrawing your own money is a waiting game. Ask someone for a favour and you get three things back, always in this order: an excuse, a promise, and — eventually, maybe — the thing you actually asked for. Cars on the street here that people in the countries that build them couldn't afford on those same incomes — I don't have an explanation for that, only the question of what it took to get there.

Forty years of learning how other places work, and I still can't stay quiet and just move through the day on autopilot the way I might need to here. Do I need any of this? No. So what keeps bringing me back? The roots. And these days, the roots keep introducing me to some very interesting people.

---

There's a religious discourse in every conversation here — right and wrong, God and the world, the same handful of lines rehearsed like a daily ritual. There's nothing wrong with religion itself; what wears me down is the debate running in place, cycling through the same territory with no real seam between what's preached and what I watch happening ten meters away. Elderly people asking for change. Children working instead of studying. Domestic work turned into something only the comfortable can afford — alongside, in the same streets, a different picture entirely: big cars, real fortunes, ease. I don't have anyone to sit down with and actually work through the question — what does the faith call for, and what's actually being done about the distance between that and daily life?

I didn't go looking for street philosophers this week. Essaouira just kept handing them to me. Three men, three days.

The first was middle-aged, poor by every visible measure — worn clothes, weathered hands — and somehow the most articulate person I spoke with all week. He said, almost word for word, what I've been circling in this chapter: the shape of what Moroccan society has become. I didn't expect that kind of clarity from someone the street would have had me write off before he opened his mouth.

The second was walking and shouting to no one — words only someone left behind would recognize. People moved past him like he wasn't there, like he was simply unwell. But some of what he was shouting was true. I couldn't unhear it.

The third was clearing a rubbish bin — someone else's mess, left by people comfortable enough to dump it and walk away. Plastic bags, building debris, camel dung, strays everywhere, a smell that catches you off guard. You learn to watch your footing here. Not every step is safe without looking down first. He looked up from the bin at one point, read something in my face, and just said: keep your peace of mind. Three words, and they landed harder than anything the other two had said.

---

I've had my share of disappointments here too — friendships that didn't hold when I needed them to. I had to make calls. Hard ones. You can't change people. People change you, if you let them.

What's carried me through it is something I picked up eleven thousand kilometres from here: "Take it easy, mate." "She'll be right, mate." Practically the whole philosophy, four words at a time. You're not responsible for what's going on in someone else's head. Keep your own energy where it needs to be. Stay who you are — don't reshape yourself just to keep someone else comfortable.

Live and let live — Paul McCartney's version, not the other one. It sounds selfish, put plainly like that. But peace of mind is the one thing Australia actually taught me, and I'm not walking it back just because I'm standing on different ground. Some days I feel less like a philosophy and more like a Green Day lyric — Jesus of Suburbia, half figured-out, getting there.

Every night before I sleep, I run the day back. Did I do wrong by anyone today. Did I say something that could've landed the wrong way. If the answer's no, that's the end of it. Anything past that isn't mine to carry.

---

Never judge, never point the finger — that's high-ranking etiquette in Australia. In Morocco, it's closer to a national pastime.

Eight of us went through high school and university together — the kind of group you don't get to build twice. AH was the best of us. Genuinely the smartest, and nobody in the group would have argued otherwise. He was part of the first generation of Moroccans who spoke fluent German, and by every measure of talent, he should have gone the furthest of any of us. His French, to this day, is better than most native French speakers I've met. Destiny had other plans. He didn't make it — not in the career sense the rest of us measure ourselves by. Staying dignified without "making it" isn't the easy road here, but he walked it anyway, and he still is that person: the one who would never ask you for anything, unlike the ones who did make it and somehow ended up with nothing left to share.

Then there's M — the other side of the same coin. He landed a post with the government, and the moment he did, he cut every tie to the friends who'd known him since before any of it.

---

Families here get torn in two — money on one side, the person on the other. Everyone has an inheritance-feud story; every family I know has one. I'm about as well-placed as anyone to talk about it: my own relationship with most of my family didn't survive it. At some point you have to make a decision. Sad, but it is what it is.

The one connection that held: three nephews, all names starting with A — the ones who never changed, who kept blood above everything else.

This is the new Morocco. Not the one they tell you it still is. Forty years of change — and sadly, I landed on the wrong side of most of it.

---

I took the tramway in Casablanca, to the part of the city where I spent most of my life — where I studied, where I taught, where my friends still live, two stops from where I was born. I got off at the wrong station. Not somewhere unfamiliar — my own backyard. And for most of that ride I'd been searching for something to recognize — a building, a street, a cinema, the football stadium — and finding nothing. Nichts. Zero. That's the moment I knew: my time in Morocco, the one I remembered, was over.

---

Not everything here is heavy. Let me cheer myself up, and you along with it: the food. Twenty Australian dollars a day doesn't stretch far back home — barely a coffee and a sandwich in Brisbane — but here it buys me a tagine delivered to my door, every single day, without exception. My wallet, calibrated in Australian dollars, has never worked this hard for me in my life. I'm not complaining.

Whatever else this place has become, it still knows how to feed you properly. That much hasn't changed in forty years, and I hope it never does.
