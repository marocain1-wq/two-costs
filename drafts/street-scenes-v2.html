Street Scenes

There's nothing that comes from nothing. And here I am. I became who I am in Australia, and now I'm bringing that person back to the country I was born in, to see how he fits. Forty years of building a life on my own terms, where if something goes wrong there's no one to blame but myself, makes for an interesting return to a place where everyone is part of everyone else's business. Five trips in three years, and the paperwork still isn't finished. I can smile about it. Morocco has taught me a kind of patience I never needed in Australia, where the only thing worth queuing for is the barista.

Essaouira, then and now

Essaouira itself has changed. It was a quiet fishing town before the tourists found it. I was sitting in a café when it struck me: where is the haik? That long white cloth, five or six metres of wool or cotton wrapped around the body from head to toe and held at the waist, was Essaouira to me. The women moved through the medina in it like white shadows, sometimes with only one eye showing. It shielded them from the wind and the sun, and from the eyes of strangers. It's in every old postcard of this town, and the guidebooks still list Essaouira as one of the last places in Morocco where you can see it. I haven't seen a single woman wearing one, not once, on any of my recent visits. The haik started to fade after independence, when women went out to work and traded it for the djellaba, and it has gone the way of the fax machine. The image I carried with me for forty years now lives only in the postcards.

Rents and everyday prices have climbed, the way I've read they have in Barcelona and Venice. Locals sell up and move out to the countryside, and the ones who stay are mostly those running businesses for the visitors. Inside the old walls, at this time of year, tourists outnumber Moroccans. The one thing I still haven't learned is how to walk past a child selling tissues to help feed their family and not carry it with me for the rest of the day.

What people say, and what they do

There's a gap I keep noticing, between what people say in public and what I see happen in private, in my own family and among people I've known my whole life. I'm not judging anyone. I'm Moroccan, I grew up inside it, and I don't have a clean answer for the gap. I just see it more clearly now that I've been away.

Money has changed things since I left. Here, a quiet current of uncertainty runs under almost every day. Withdrawing your own money is a waiting game. Ask someone for a favour and you get three things back, always in this order: an excuse, a promise and, eventually, maybe, the thing you actually asked for. And on the streets there are cars that people in the countries that build them couldn't afford on the same incomes. I don't have an explanation for that, only curiosity about how they got there. Luckily, none of it touches me much. My income and my life are in Australia, so I get to watch all this as an observer, not a player.

Forty years of learning how other places work, and I still can't switch to autopilot here and let the day carry me. Do I need any of this? No. So what keeps bringing me back? The roots. And lately, the roots keep introducing me to some very interesting people.

Three men, three days

Religion comes up in almost every conversation here: right and wrong, God and the world, the same few lines repeated like a daily ritual. I have nothing against religion itself. What puzzles me is how rarely the talk meets the street, where elderly people ask for change, children work instead of studying, and domestic help is something only the comfortable can afford. And a few metres away there's a different picture entirely: big cars, real fortunes, an easy life. I'd love to sit down with someone and really work through it. What does the faith ask of us, and what are we actually doing about the distance between that and daily life?

Maybe that's why I noticed them. I didn't go looking for street philosophers this week. Essaouira kept handing them to me anyway. Three men, three days.

The first was middle-aged and poor by every visible measure: worn clothes, weathered hands. He was also the most articulate person I spoke to all week. He described, almost word for word, what I've been trying to put down in this chapter: what Moroccan society has become. The street would have told me to write him off before he opened his mouth. I'm glad I didn't listen.

The second walked along shouting to no one, in words only someone left behind would recognise. People stepped around him as if he were simply unwell. But some of what he shouted was true, and I couldn't unhear it.

The third was clearing out a rubbish bin, someone else's mess, left by people comfortable enough to dump it and walk away: plastic bags, building rubble, camel dung, strays everywhere, a smell that catches you off guard. Here you learn to watch your step. He looked up at one point, read something in my face and said: keep your peace of mind. Five words, and they stayed with me longer than anything the other two had said.

Peace of mind

Not every surprise here has been a good one. Some friendships didn't hold when I needed them to, and I had to make some hard calls. You can't change people. People change you, if you let them.

What carries me through is something I picked up seventeen thousand kilometres from here: "Take it easy, mate." "She'll be right, mate." Practically a whole philosophy, four words at a time. You're not responsible for what goes on in someone else's head. Keep your energy where it belongs. Stay who you are, and don't reshape yourself just to keep someone else comfortable.

Live and let live. Not Paul McCartney's version, the other one. Put plainly like that, it sounds selfish. But peace of mind is the one thing Australia really taught me, and I'm not giving it up just because I'm standing on different ground. Some days I feel less like a philosophy and more like a Green Day song: Jesus of Suburbia, half figured out, getting there.

Every night before I sleep, I run the day back. Did I do wrong by anyone today? Did I say something that could have landed the wrong way? If the answer is no, that's the end of it. Anything past that isn't mine to carry.

Old friends

Never judge, never point the finger: in Australia, that's basic etiquette. In Morocco, it's closer to a national sport.

Eight of us went through high school and university together, the kind of group you don't get to build twice. AH was the best of us. Genuinely the smartest, and nobody in the group would have argued otherwise. He was part of the first generation of Moroccans who spoke fluent German, and by every measure of talent he should have gone the furthest. His French, to this day, is better than most native speakers I've met. Destiny had other plans. He never had the kind of career the rest of us measure ourselves by. Staying dignified without "making it" isn't the easy road here, but he walked it, and he still does. He's the one who would never ask you for anything, unlike some who did make it and somehow ended up with nothing to share.

Then there's M, the other side of the same coin. He landed a government post, and the moment he did, he cut every tie with the friends who'd known him since before any of it.

Family

Families here get split in two: money on one side, people on the other. Everyone has an inheritance story; every family I know has one, and mine is no exception. My relationship with most of my family didn't survive it. At some point you have to make a decision. Sad, but it is what it is.

What held were my three nephews, all with names starting with A. They never changed, and they kept family above everything else.

This is the new Morocco, not the one people still tell you about. Forty years of change, and I'm seeing most of it from the outside.

Casablanca

I took the tramway in Casablanca, to the part of the city where I spent most of my life: where I studied, where I taught, where my friends still live, two stops from where I was born. I got off at the wrong station. Not somewhere unfamiliar, my own backyard. For most of that ride I'd been looking for something I recognised, a building, a street, a cinema, the football stadium, and found nothing. Nichts. Zero. That's when I knew the Morocco I remembered was gone. The one in front of me is a different country, and I'm still getting to know it.

And the food

Not everything here is serious. Let me cheer us both up: the food. Twenty Australian dollars a day doesn't stretch far back home, barely a coffee and a sandwich in Brisbane, but here it buys me a tagine delivered to my door, every single day, without exception. My wallet, calibrated in Australian dollars, has never worked this hard for me. I'm not complaining.

Whatever else this place has become, it still knows how to feed you properly. That much hasn't changed in forty years, and I hope it never does.
